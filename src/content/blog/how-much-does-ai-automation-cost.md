Search for this question and you will mostly find either a vague "it depends" or a suspiciously neat price list. We would rather tell you why it depends, so you can judge a quote when you get one.

## What moves the price

**How many systems are involved.** An automation that moves information between two tools you already use is a smaller job than one that has to read an email, check a spreadsheet, update your accounts package and notify three people. Every extra system is another thing to connect, test and keep working.

**How much judgement it needs.** "When a form arrives, copy it into the CRM" is mechanical. "Read this supplier email, work out whether it is an invoice, a query or a complaint, and draft a sensible reply" needs AI, and AI needs testing against the awkward real-world cases, not just the tidy ones.

**How messy the inputs are.** Clean, consistent data is cheap to automate. PDFs from twelve suppliers in twelve formats, or a spreadsheet only one person understands, is not. Part of the job is sometimes tidying the input first.

**How long it has to keep working.** An automation that runs once is different from one your business will rely on every day for years. Testing, error handling, documentation and support are what make the second kind dependable, and they are worth paying for.

## What else you will pay for

The build is not the only cost. Be ready for:

- **Tool subscriptions** for the platform the automation runs on, such as n8n, Zapier or Make. Pricing models differ and change, so check the current pricing pages rather than trusting an old blog post, including this one.
- **AI usage**, if the automation calls a model such as Claude or ChatGPT. This is usually modest for small-business volumes, but it is a real line item.
- **Hosting**, if the automation or tool is self-hosted.
- **Support and changes**, because your business will change and the automation has to change with it.

## What a good quote includes

- A plain-English description of what will be built, and what is deliberately left out.
- What "done" means, so you can tell when it has been delivered.
- Testing, documentation and a handover, not just the build.
- What support is included afterwards, and what happens when it breaks.
- Who owns it at the end.

If a quote does not cover those, ask. If someone quotes a number before asking about your process, ask how they got it.

## How to get a real figure

1. Write down the job as a list of steps, as if explaining it to a new starter.
2. Note which tools are involved and who does each step today.
3. Estimate how often it happens and how long it takes. This is also how you work out what it is worth to automate.
4. Talk to someone who will build it.
